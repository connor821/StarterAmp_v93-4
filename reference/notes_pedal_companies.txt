### The big feature I'd borrow: parallel processing

Beam Splitter is the clearest reference for an effect that produces several saturated versions of a signal in parallel. - Parameter smoothing to avoid clicks when changing routing or blend.

### The most important lesson: fuzz is a circuit, not a preset

Analog Man explicitly describes how germanium Fuzz Face performance depends on transistor gain, leakage, noise, and device-to-device variation. It also notes the sensitivity of classic fuzz circuits to guitar volume and the effects placed before them.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.analogman.com\&sz=32)

Analog Man

+1



That suggests several features for your existing transistor-modeling work:

- Per-device parameters: gain, leakage, saturation current, and temperature response.
- Bias controls: allow the operating point to change the amount of sustain, compression, and gating.
- Pickup and guitar-volume interaction: don't model fuzz as though it always receives a fixed, low-impedance signal.
- Input-impedance interaction: preserve the way a vintage fuzz responds differently to a guitar pickup versus a buffered effect.
- Component tolerances: allow meaningful differences between individual simulated units.

You don't need to simulate every transistor's microscopic physics at maximum complexity. A carefully calibrated reduced-order transistor model can give you the behavior that matters without overwhelming the CPU.

My priority: implement a strong Fuzz Face-style circuit model with germanium and silicon variants, then build several distinct vintage fuzz topologies on the same transistor-modeling foundation. That gives you more value than adding a dozen superficial fuzz presets.

A. A proper tape-echo engine. Don't merely low-pass each repeat. Include a feedback path, saturation in the loop, modulation, head configuration, and time-dependent coloration. Those interactions are what make the effect feel like a tape machine.

B. A double-tracker. This is particularly useful for rhythm guitars and studio-style widening. Model a short, controllable delay with pitch drift and saturation. Make it possible to transition from subtle thickening to obvious flanging.

C. A family of delay engines. Use distinct algorithms rather than one algorithm with a dropdown of presets:

- Clean digital delay
- Dual digital delay
- BBD-style analog delay
- Tape echo
- Multi-head drum echo
- Reverse delay
- Modulated delay
- Granular or glitch delay

D. A high-quality reverb suite. Prioritize spring, plate, room, hall, shimmer, and evolving ambience. Spring should be a dedicated model, not a generic reverb with a spring preset label.

E. Stereo that is genuinely stereo. Use independent delay times, modulation, early reflections, or reverb structures where the effect calls for them. Avoid simply duplicating a mono signal with a few milliseconds of offset.

For a plugin, the Strymon-inspired feature I would put near the top of the roadmap is a tape/double-tracking block. It can be useful with clean, crunch, and high-gain amps alike, without needing to change the amp model.

### My top Eventide-inspired features

1\. MicroPitch-style stereo widening

This is particularly useful for modern high-gain guitar. Use two pitch-shifted voices, for example one slightly flat and one slightly sharp, with small independent delays. The values should be adjustable rather than fixed.

Include:

- Independent left/right detuning.
- Independent delay times.
- Wet/dry balance.
- Stereo width.
- Optional modulation.
- Mono compatibility controls to reduce unwanted cancellation when summed.

This is more flexible than a simple chorus and can create a wide lead tone without obvious cyclic movement.

2\. UltraTap-style rhythmic delay

A multi-tap delay can create rhythmic subdivisions, repeated attacks, and dense textures without needing several separate delay plugins. Add per-tap level and pan, a tap-count control, feedback, and a smooth transition between ordinary echoes and diffuse, reverb-like textures.

3\. Polyphonic pitch shifting

If your current pitch effects are monophonic or octave-only, this is one of the more valuable upgrades. Prioritize:

- Octave up/down.
- Two- and four-part harmonies.
- Diatonic harmony modes.
- Pitch bend and whammy-style control.
- Detuning and stereo doubling.
- Fast tracking with low latency.

Polyphonic pitch shifting is computationally demanding, so it should be an independently optimized module with its own latency and quality options.

4\. Freeze and infinite sustain

Allow a reverb or granular buffer to capture a moment and sustain it while the player continues performing. This works especially well for ambient intros, instrumental metal transitions, and layered guitar arrangements.

5\. Modular effect routing

The H90 model is worth studying at the architectural level: two effects in a program, with series or parallel routing and the ability to insert other processing into the chain. You can implement a more plugin-native version using your existing effect modules.&#x20;

## 8. The most important effects to get technically right

For your hybrid DSP/circuit-modeling approach, I'd separate the effects into three implementation classes.

| Effect class                   | Best implementation approach                        | Critical details                                                                             |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Analog-inspired drive and fuzz | Nonlinear circuit model or calibrated nonlinear DSP | Clipping topology, frequency-dependent feedback, bias, input impedance, gain staging         |
| Vintage modulation and delay   | Purpose-built DSP algorithms                        | Delay-line filtering, modulation, feedback-path saturation, clock/time behavior              |
| Modern spatial effects         | High-quality algorithmic DSP                        | Diffusion, feedback matrices, stereo decorrelation, modulation, damping, parameter smoothing |

A few specific engineering points matter more than adding more product names to the interface:

- Oversampling: prioritize nonlinear distortion, fuzz, clipping, and saturation paths. Use suitable filtering and measure aliasing.
- Feedback loops: put the intended filtering, saturation, and modulation inside the loop where the reference effect requires it. Applying a filter only to the final output won't reproduce the same behavior.
- Automation: smooth parameter changes and avoid allocating memory in the audio callback. This is especially important for delays, granular effects, and changing effect configurations.
- Latency: document latency introduced by pitch-shifting and look-ahead algorithms. Provide quality/latency trade-offs where needed.
- Stereo: implement genuine stereo processing where it improves the effect, and test mono summing.
- Preset design: expose useful musical controls by default, with optional advanced parameters for people who want deeper control.

## 10. A final caution about research and product identity

Use these pedals as references for what an effect does and how it is controlled. Don't assume a marketing description tells you the actual circuit topology, component values, or DSP implementation.
