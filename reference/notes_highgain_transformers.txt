Yes — but reliable high-gain amp transformer data is much harder to find than schematic data. For your amp simulator, the key distinction is between:

- Documented transformer specifications — primary impedance, turns ratio, winding resistance, inductance, and power rating.
- Measured transformer behavior — frequency-dependent impedance, leakage inductance, capacitance, saturation, and losses.
- Original manufacturer data — often incomplete for guitar amps, particularly for discontinued models.

For high-gain amps like the Peavey 5150, Mesa/Boogie Dual Rectifier, Soldano SLO-100, Diezel VH4, and ENGL Savage, I would not assume that a schematic or a replacement-transformer listing gives you enough information to reproduce the original output-transformer response. The most useful data tends to come from transformer manufacturers, service documentation, and measurements of original hardware.

## What I would implement in StarterAmp

You don't need complete factory data for every amp to build a useful transformer model. I'd use a hierarchy of confidence:

| Model family             | Recommended approach                                                            |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SLO-100                  | Start from published replacement-transformer values; label as a proxy           |
| 5150 / 6505              | Separate profile, constrained by the specific output stage; measure if possible |
| Dual Rectifier           | Separate profile; account for operating point and output-stage configuration    |
| VH4 / Savage             | Start with estimated parameters and calibrate against recordings or hardware    |
| Generic modern high-gain | Parameterized transformer family with documented assumptions                    |

For each profile, prioritize:

1. Turns ratio and reflected primary impedance — including the intended speaker tap.
2. Primary winding resistance and magnetizing inductance — both affect low-frequency response and output-stage loading.
3. Leakage inductance and winding capacitance — important for high-frequency behavior and resonances.
4. Core saturation and hysteresis — particularly when driving high output levels and low frequencies.
5. Output-stage negative feedback — because the transformer, speaker load, and feedback loop interact.

A useful reference for the equivalent-circuit approach is this [Mercury Magnetics transformer-model explanation](https://www.mercurymagnetics.com/wp-content/uploads/woocommerce_uploads/2018/06/EVJ-Mercury-Upgrade-V2-3-PCB-Manual-V2.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com), which illustrates primary and secondary resistance, magnetizing and leakage inductance, capacitances, and core-loss terms.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.mercurymagnetics.com\&sz=32)

Mercury Magnetics



My recommendation: treat the SLO-100 as your first data-backed high-gain transformer profile, but don't call its parameters an original factory specification. For the 5150, Rectifier, VH4, and Savage, I would keep the profiles distinct while marking their transformer parameters as estimated until measured or documented data becomes available. A shared generic transformer model with different parameters is a better foundation than inventing supposedly exact specifications for each amp.
