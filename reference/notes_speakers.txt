For StarterAmp, this is a worthwhile next step: speaker impedance curves and Thiele–Small (T/S) parameters can make the speaker and power-amp interaction more realistic, particularly when the simulated output stage has finite output impedance or negative feedback.

One important distinction: a speaker's T/S parameters describe its low-frequency electromechanical behavior, but they do not fully characterize its guitar-speaker sound. The V30's midrange emphasis, a Greenback's breakup, and a Celestion Blue's cone compression require additional frequency-response, nonlinear, and level-dependent data.

I'd build a speaker dataset with three layers:

1. Electrical/mechanical parameters: \\(R_e, L_e, F_s, Q\_{es}, Q\_{ms}, Q\_{ts}, V\_{as,}\\) and, where available, \\(B\ell, M\_{ms}, C\_{ms}\\), and \\(S_d\\).
2. Measured impedance versus frequency: complex impedance, including resistance and reactance, rather than just nominal 8 Ω or 16 Ω.
3. Guitar-speaker behavior: on-axis/off-axis response, breakup modes, compression, and response changes with drive level.

## 2. How to get actual impedance-versus-frequency curves

For the Celestions, I would not try to infer a complete curve from the published \\(F_s\\) and \\(R_e\\) values alone. Instead, pursue three routes.

[How to measure speaker impedance](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/djjL__VIowOcZMHMVDCWp66VXs9Dd5y9Cm0aIjFOfpLtDkP1tUM37eAqmtVYI34lmiZa4WsGOB-BlyI-nAw5UWgS5p-brfH-b_hFwE80VZNr-fgdeXJnqKoXKQ0Rukv5seYp8VWn2wuoCnYF3Fy-6gsL0EgheoTfm_6A9CaczK0?purpose=inline)

Route A — measure a physical speaker

Highest confidence

Measure complex impedance across roughly 20 Hz–20 kHz using a calibrated impedance fixture or analyzer. Save both real and imaginary components, not only magnitude. If possible, measure multiple samples of each model.

A useful technical reference is Analog Devices' [AN-843 speaker impedance measurement application note](https://www.analog.com/media/en/technical-documentation/application-notes/236037846AN_843.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com).&#x20;

Route C — fit a model to published parameters

Where physical measurements aren't available, build a low-frequency electromechanical model from published T/S parameters and fit a high-frequency electrical model to measured or published impedance data.

Mark these profiles as estimated until validated. A fitted curve can be a useful engineering approximation without being an original factory measurement.

One important limitation: a single small-signal impedance sweep will not capture the full dynamic behavior of a guitar speaker driven hard. You will eventually want measurements at multiple drive levels and, ideally, different temperatures.

## 3. How I'd model the speaker in StarterAmp

I'd separate the electrical load model from the acoustic speaker model. They interact, but they serve different purposes.

Tube or solid-state output stage

Output impedance, feedback, power-stage clipping

Complex speaker-load model

Impedance peak · voice-coil inductance · phase · cabinet loading

Electromechanical speaker dynamics

Cone displacement · motor force · suspension · compression · breakup

Cabinet, microphone, and acoustic response

Frequency response · directivity · enclosure behavior · IR processing

### A. Electrical load model

At minimum, represent the speaker impedance as a complex, frequency-dependent load:

\\[ Z(f)=R(f)+jX(f) \\]

For the low-frequency resonance, the T/S equivalent circuit is a useful starting point. Above resonance, voice-coil inductance and losses become increasingly important. For guitar speakers, additional resonances and cone modes may require a fitted network or measured data.

This model lets the output stage respond to the load instead of seeing a fixed resistor. That can change output-stage frequency response, damping, and the amount of feedback correction.

### B. Nonlinear speaker behavior

Add these features in increasing order of complexity:

1. Voice-coil heating, which increases resistance under sustained power.
2. Power compression, calibrated from measurements rather than a generic compressor curve.
3. Suspension and motor nonlinearity at larger excursions.
4. Cone breakup modes, including their level-dependent behavior.
5. Alnico-related compression behavior where supported by measurements.

For the Blue, pay particular attention to level-dependent compression. For the V30, don't reduce its signature midrange to a single EQ bump; its acoustic response and breakup behavior are part of its identity.

### C. Cabinet and multiple-speaker loading

Don't treat the impedance curve of a single speaker as the impedance curve of every cabinet containing it.

- Four identical speakers in parallel have one-quarter the impedance of a single speaker at each frequency, assuming identical loading.
- Four in series have four times the impedance.
- Mixed speaker cabinets need each speaker's electrical and acoustic contribution modeled separately.
- A sealed cabinet changes the low-frequency system behavior; an open-back combo has a different acoustic loading and radiation pattern.

The electrical load seen by the amp also depends on the wiring configuration and the cabinet, not just the speaker model.

## 4. Recommended data format

I'd store one record per exact speaker model, revision, and nominal impedance, rather than one generic record per family.

```

{
  "model": "Celestion Vintage 30",
  "revision": "unspecified",
  "nominal_impedance_ohm": 8,
  "power_rating_w": 60,
  "sensitivity_db_1w_1m": 100,
  "small_signal": {
    "re_ohm": 7.3,
    "fs_hz": 75,
    "qes": null,
    "qms": null,
    "qts": null,
    "vas_l": null,
    "le_mh": null,
    "bl_tm": null,
    "mms_g": null,
    "cms_um_n": null,
    "sd_cm2": null
  },
  "impedance_data": {
    "format": "frequency_hz, real_ohm, imag_ohm",
    "source": null,
    "measured": false
  },
  "acoustic_data": {
    "frequency_response_source": "manufacturer_graph",
    "off_axis_data": null,
    "level_dependent_data": null
  },
  "confidence": "partial_manufacturer_data"
}

```

The `null` values are intentional: they should remain unknown until you find credible data or measure them. Don't fill in the missing Celestion parameters using Jensen data or another speaker's specifications.

## My recommended order of work

1. Implement the electrical load model and allow each amp profile to interact with it.
2. Add the complete published T/S profiles for Jensen C12N, P12N, and C12K as your first fully parameterized test cases.
3. Add the Celestion V30, Greenback, G12H, and Blue profiles with their verified \\(R_e\\) and \\(F_s\\), then obtain or measure the remaining parameters.
4. Validate the electrical load interaction with a representative tube amp and a solid-state amp.
5. Add power compression and nonlinear cone behavior only after the small-signal behavior is credible.
6. Keep the acoustic cabinet and microphone IR system separate so the same speaker dynamics can be tested with different cabinets and microphone positions.

The key payoff: your speaker models could affect the simulated amplifier's response before the cabinet/microphone stage. That's much more convincing than applying a speaker EQ curve after the amp. For a hybrid amp simulator, it is also a meaningful differentiator from products that use a fixed load assumption and rely entirely on static cabinet IRs.
