For your amp simulator, the most valuable mic data is the actual frequency-response curve at multiple distances, not just the published frequency range. Since your speaker models roll off around 4–6 kHz, the mic response is effectively your final major tonal-shaping stage: presence peaks, upper-treble roll-off, and low-frequency proximity behavior will have a large effect on how realistic each mic sounds.

I found several useful manufacturer sources, including a particularly valuable Beyerdynamic document that plots the M 160 at 10 cm and 1 m on the same graph. That is exactly the sort of data that can help you model distance-dependent bass response rather than using an arbitrary low-shelf boost. Technical Specifications +1

### 2\. Which curves you can actually use

| Microphone | Response data                                      | Distance/proximity data                                               |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SM57       | Manufacturer graph to 20 kHz axis; rated to 15 kHz | General cardioid guidance; no verified model-specific distance family |
| SM7B       | Manufacturer graph to 20 kHz                       | Switch settings, not a verified distance family                       |
| MD 421-II  | Direct graph still to be verified                  | Bass-control behavior; distance family not verified                   |
| R-121      | Manufacturer graph; rated to 15 kHz                | Generic bidirectional ribbon example in manual                        |
| M 160      | Manufacturer graph to 20 kHz                       | 10 cm and 1 m curves                                                  |
| e906       | Manufacturer graph to 20 kHz axis; rated to 18 kHz | Presence switch settings; distance family not verified                |
| C414 XLS   | Manufacturer response plots to 20 kHz              | Pattern and bass-cut settings; distance family not verified           |

One important distinction: a graph with a 20 kHz horizontal axis does not mean the microphone has a specified response all the way to 20 kHz. For example, Royer specifies the R-121 to 15 kHz, while Beyerdynamic specifies the M 160 to 18 kHz. Royer Labs +1

### 3\. How I'd use this in your DSP engine

I'd prioritize the following implementation:

* Static measured response: digitize each manufacturer's graph into frequency/gain points, then interpolate in log-frequency space. Store a separate curve for each switch setting.

* Distance-dependent bass: implement proximity as a separate low-frequency response component. Start with the M 160's two measured distances as a reference, but don't assume its behavior transfers exactly to a cardioid SM57.

* Upper-treble behavior: preserve each mic's actual response through 15–20 kHz where data supports it. Don't artificially extend a rated bandwidth with a made-up peak.

* Cab interaction: apply the mic response after the speaker/cab response. This will let the mic shape the remaining 4–6 kHz roll-off and any surviving upper harmonics without compensating for the speaker model twice.

* Validation: compare the resulting curves against the source plots and test the complete speaker-plus-mic response, not just the isolated mic filter.

My recommendation: start with the SM57, e906, R-121 and M 160. Together, they give you four distinct tonal archetypes, and the M 160 provides the strongest manufacturer-supplied distance data found here. Add the SM7B and C414 next for smoother dynamic and condenser options.

For the best results, treat these manufacturer graphs as reference measurements, not perfect calibration data: published curves may be typical rather than individually measured, and test conditions differ between manufacturers.
